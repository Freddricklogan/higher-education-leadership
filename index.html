Re Higher Education Leadership & Governance

American higher education is a diverse ecology of institutions run through shared governance, tuition-driven finance, and a dense legal framework.

  • The sector spans six institution types — from open-access community colleges to research universities — each with a distinct mission, funding model, and governance structure.
  • Shared governance divides authority among the governing board (fiduciary), the administration (executive), and the faculty (academic), with primary responsibility assigned by expertise.
  • Leaders read situations through Bolman & Deal's four frames and lead adaptive, not merely technical, change across a loosely coupled system.
  • Finance turns on net tuition revenue: declining state support and tuition discounting make affordability the defining problem of the era.
  • Student development theory — Chickering, Astin, Tinto, and others — grounds the co-curriculum that drives belonging, learning, and persistence.
  • Every decision sits inside legal domains — Title IX, FERPA, ADA, due process — and is validated externally through assessment and accreditation.
Orientation

The U.S. higher education landscape

American higher education is not one system but a diverse ecology of institutions, differentiated by mission, control, selectivity, and the students they serve. The Carnegie Classification is the standard framework for describing this diversity.

Ways institutions differ

  • Control: public, private nonprofit, or private for-profit
  • Level: two-year (associate) vs. four-year and graduate
  • Mission: teaching, research, access, or a blend
  • Selectivity & residentiality: open-access to highly selective
  • Special mission: Minority-Serving Institutions, religious, single-sex, tribal

Governance & administration

Who decides — and how authority is shared

Colleges are governed by a distinctive arrangement called shared governance, in which authority is divided among the governing board, the administration, and the faculty. Understanding who holds which authority is the key to leading in higher education.

Governing board

Fiduciary authority

Trustees or regents hold ultimate legal responsibility: they set mission and policy, hire and evaluate the president, approve the budget, and steward assets — but do not manage day-to-day operations.

Administration

Executive authority

The president and their team implement policy, manage operations, finance, and personnel, and represent the institution externally. They are accountable to the board.

Faculty

Academic authority

Through the senate and departments, faculty hold primary authority over curriculum, degree requirements, research, and — via peer review — appointment, promotion, and tenure.

Academic vs. administrative authority

  • Academic authority rests on expertise and peer judgment — collegial, decentralized, slow by design.
  • Administrative authority rests on position in a hierarchy — managerial, centralized, faster.
  • Birnbaum calls the university an "organized anarchy" and a "loosely coupled" system where the two logics coexist in tension.

The politics & policy of governance

  • External actors — legislatures, system offices, accreditors, donors, unions — all shape decisions.
  • Bolman & Deal's four frames (structural, human resource, political, symbolic) help leaders read a situation.
  • Governance failures usually trace to unclear authority, weak trust, or bypassed process — not bad people.
The 1966 AAUP Statement on Government of Colleges and Universities — jointly formulated with the ACE and AGB — is the foundational articulation of shared governance and the appropriate spheres of board, administration, and faculty authority.
Governance · in depth

Shared governance, role by role

Shared governance is not a single committee — it is a division of labor among distinct offices, each with a legitimate sphere of authority. The AAUP frames it as joint effort, with primary responsibility allocated to whoever holds the greatest stake and expertise in a given decision.

Board of trustees

Fiduciary stewards

Hold the institution in trust: set mission and broad policy, hire/evaluate/dismiss the president, approve the budget and major commitments, and safeguard assets. Public boards are typically appointed by governors (or elected); private boards are usually self-perpetuating. Duties of care, loyalty, and obedience govern their conduct. Boards should steer, not row — govern policy, not manage operations.

President / Chancellor

Chief executive

The board's single employee and the institution's public face. Executes policy, leads the cabinet, owns budget and personnel, manages crises and external relations (legislators, donors, media, alumni), and serves at the board's pleasure. Average presidential tenure has fallen for decades, raising continuity risk.

Provost

Chief academic officer

The number-two executive and steward of the academic core: oversees deans, faculty hiring, promotion and tenure, curriculum, and the academic budget. Often the internal-facing counterpart to an external-facing president — and the single most common pipeline to a presidency.

Faculty senate

Academic voice

The representative body through which faculty exercise primary authority over curriculum, degree requirements, and academic standards, and advise on budget and appointments. Its power is real but largely consultative — recommendations the board or administration can, in principle, override.

The AAUP conception of shared governance

  • Articulated in the 1966 Statement on Government of Colleges and Universities (AAUP, ACE, AGB).
  • Primary responsibility is assigned by expertise: faculty over curriculum and academic standards; the board over finances and assets; broad consultation on the rest.
  • Governance is joint effort — interdependent, requiring communication and mutual respect, not silos.
  • Presidential and decanal appointments should include meaningful faculty consultation.

Where the tensions live

  • Speed vs. legitimacy: administrators can act fast; senates confer legitimacy but slowly.
  • Consultation vs. co-decision: faculty often expect a vote where administrators offer only input.
  • Contingent faculty: most instructors are now off the tenure track and largely outside senate power.
  • Corporatization: critics warn that managerial logics and assertive boards can erode faculty primacy over academics.
A useful test. When a decision is contested, ask: whose expertise does this decision most depend on? That is usually who should hold primary responsibility — the core insight of shared governance done well.
Interactive · Leadership lenses

Bolman & Deal's four frames of leadership

Lee Bolman and Terrence Deal argue that effective leaders reframe — reading the same situation through four different lenses, because each frame reveals what the others hide. Below, all four are applied to one scenario: a university closing a low-enrollment academic department.

Structural frame — the factory

Sees organizations as rational systems of roles, goals, policies, and coordination. Leadership means designing the right structure, clarifying responsibility, and aligning process with goals.

Roles & rulesGoals & metricsCoordination
  • Core questionIs the structure aligned to the goal?
  • MetaphorFactory / machine
  • On the closureRun the enrollment, cost, and completion data; follow program-discontinuance policy and the teach-out procedure precisely.
  • Blind spotTreats people as roles — misses grief, fear, and politics.

Human resource frame — the family

Sees the fit between people and organization: needs, skills, feelings, and growth. Leadership means empowering, supporting, and investing in people so they and the institution both thrive.

Needs & growthEmpowermentSupport
  • Core questionHow do we meet people's needs?
  • MetaphorFamily
  • On the closureHold listening sessions, protect affected faculty with reassignment or retraining, and cushion the human cost.
  • Blind spotCan avoid hard tradeoffs and underweight power and structure.

Political frame — the jungle

Sees organizations as arenas of scarce resources and competing interests, where power, coalitions, bargaining, and conflict are normal and enduring. Leadership means mapping power and negotiating.

Power & interestsCoalitionsBargaining
  • Core questionWho has power, and what do they want?
  • MetaphorJungle / arena
  • On the closureMap stakeholders, build a coalition with deans and the senate, and negotiate before the vote.
  • Blind spotCan become cynical and neglect shared purpose.

Symbolic frame — the theater

Sees culture, ritual, story, and meaning. Organizations are held together by shared symbols and beliefs, not just structure. Leadership means shaping meaning and honoring what matters.

CultureRitual & storyMeaning
  • Core questionWhat does this mean to the community?
  • MetaphorTheater / temple
  • On the closureHold a ceremony honoring the department's legacy so people can grieve and find meaning in the transition.
  • Blind spotSymbolism without substance rings hollow and can mask inaction.

Leadership theory

Four leadership theories in higher education

No single style fits the loosely coupled, expertise-driven university. These four theories dominate contemporary higher-education leadership scholarship, each answering "how does a leader move a campus?" differently.

Transformational

Burns & Bass

Leaders raise followers' motivation and morality toward a shared vision — through idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration. Contrasts with transactional (exchange-based) leadership.

Servant

Greenleaf

The leader serves first — prioritizing the growth, well-being, and empowerment of followers and community. A strong fit with the ethic of care in student affairs and mission-driven institutions.

Adaptive

Heifetz & Linsky

Distinguishes technical problems (known fixes) from adaptive challenges (which demand new learning, changed values, and loss). Leaders regulate distress, give the work back to the people, and mobilize others to face hard realities.

Distributed / shared

Spillane; Kezar & Holcombe

Leadership is a property of the system, not one person — stretched across many actors, roles, and interactions. Well-matched to shared governance, where authority is genuinely dispersed across faculty and administrators.

Adaptive leadership and higher ed. Most campus crises — declining enrollment, equity gaps, budget shortfalls — are adaptive, not technical. Heifetz's warning is that leaders fail when they apply a technical fix (a new policy, more money) to a problem that actually requires changing values and behavior.
Financing postsecondary education

Where the money comes from — and where it goes

Institutions blend several revenue streams, each with its own constraints. Public disinvestment has shifted cost toward students, making affordability the defining finance problem of the era. Try allocating a budget below.

Tuition & fees

Net, not sticker

Published price minus institutional aid (discounting). The largest revenue source at most tuition-dependent institutions.

State appropriations

Public support

Direct state funding to public institutions — declining per-student over decades, driving tuition up.

Endowment & gifts

Philanthropy

Invested gifts whose returns fund operations and aid; often restricted. Highly concentrated at wealthy institutions.

Grants & auxiliary

Research & enterprise

Sponsored research (federal/foundation) plus auxiliaries — housing, dining, athletics — that are largely self-supporting.

The affordability problem

  • Declining state support per student shifts cost onto tuition and loans.
  • "Sticker" vs. "net" price: high published prices deter students even when aid lowers the real cost.
  • Student debt now exceeds $1.7 trillion, with unequal burdens by race and income.
  • The "iron triangle": cost, quality, and access are hard to improve simultaneously.

The business model

Enrollment management & net tuition revenue

For most institutions, students are the business model: net tuition revenue funds the enterprise. Strategic enrollment management (SEM) — a field pioneered by Don Hossler — integrates marketing, admissions, financial aid, and retention to manage the flow of students, and the revenue they bring.

The enrollment funnel

Each stage is a smaller slice of the one above it. Managing the funnel means improving conversion at every step — and the yield rate (enrolled ÷ admits) is the number that keeps admissions deans awake.

Tuition discounting & net revenue

  • Sticker price is the published tuition — almost no one at a private college pays it.
  • Institutional aid (grants/scholarships from the institution's own funds) discounts that price.
  • The tuition discount rate = institutional aid ÷ gross tuition revenue; at many private colleges it now exceeds 50% for first-year students.
  • Net tuition revenue = what the institution actually keeps after discounting — the number that funds operations.

Bowen's revenue theory of cost

  • Howard Bowen's "revenue theory of cost": institutions raise all the money they can, and spend all the money they raise.
  • The dominant goals — excellence, prestige, influence — are insatiable, so there is no natural ceiling on spending.
  • Cost per student is therefore driven by the revenue available, not by what education "must" cost.
  • This helps explain the amenities race, administrative growth, and why cost is so hard to cut.
The discounting trap. Raising aid to boost enrollment can lift headcount while lowering net revenue — filling seats but shrinking the budget. The core SEM discipline is optimizing net tuition revenue, not just the raw number of students.
College student development

How students grow — the theories that guide student affairs

Student development theory explains how college students change intellectually, socially, and personally, and why environment and involvement matter. These frameworks underpin advising, residence life, and retention work.

Psychosocial

Chickering — Seven Vectors

Development along seven vectors: competence, managing emotions, autonomy toward interdependence, mature relationships, identity, purpose, and integrity.

Involvement

Astin — Involvement / I-E-O

Learning is proportional to the quality and quantity of student involvement. The Inputs–Environment–Outcomes model frames how college affects students.

Retention

Tinto — Departure / Integration

Persistence depends on academic and social integration into the institution; a lack of integration predicts departure.

Cognitive

Perry — Intellectual Development

Students move from dualism (right/wrong) through multiplicity and relativism toward committed, contextual reasoning.

Self-authorship

Baxter Magolda — Self-Authorship

The capacity to internally define beliefs, identity, and relationships — moving from external formulas to an internal voice.

Identity

Identity development models

Domain-specific models — racial (Cross, Helms), ethnic, gender, and sexual identity — describe growth in social identity and belonging.

Why it matters for leaders: the environment is the lever administrators can actually pull. Astin and Tinto together argue that designing for involvement and integration — not just admitting the "right" students — is what drives learning and persistence.
Student affairs

The functional areas of student affairs

Student affairs operationalizes development theory into services. These functional areas — usually under a Vice President for Student Affairs or Dean of Students — form the co-curriculum that supports retention, wellbeing, and belonging.

Academic advising

Guidance

Course selection, degree planning, and early-alert intervention — consistently one of the strongest predictors of persistence.

Residence life

Housing

On-campus living, resident assistants, and living-learning communities that build community and support development.

Counseling

Mental health

Clinical services, crisis response, and wellness programming — amid a documented surge in student demand.

Career services

Outcomes

Career counseling, internships, and employer relations that connect the curriculum to the world of work.

Student conduct

Community standards

The code of conduct and disciplinary process — grounded in due process and educational, not merely punitive, sanctions.

Diversity & inclusion

Belonging

Cultural centers and identity-based programming that foster inclusion and a sense of belonging.

Disability services

Access

Accommodations under the ADA and §504 that ensure equal access to programs, facilities, and testing.

Health & basic needs

Wellbeing

Health services plus food pantries and emergency aid addressing housing and food insecurity.

The co-curriculum is not extracurricular. Astin and Tinto's research reframes these services as central to learning and persistence — the environment administrators design, not add-ons around the "real" academic work.
Diversity, equity & inclusion

Access, inequality, and campus climate

Higher education both reflects and can reproduce social stratification. Equity work asks who gets in, who succeeds, and whether the campus environment allows every student to thrive.

Access & stratification

  • Opportunity is stratified by race, income, and first-generation status — from application through completion.
  • Selective institutions enroll disproportionately affluent students; access institutions carry the equity load with fewer resources.
  • Gaps appear at every stage: enrollment, persistence, completion, debt, and post-college outcomes.

The role of Minority-Serving Institutions

  • MSIs (HBCUs, HSIs, TCUs) educate a large share of students of color and drive social mobility.
  • They demonstrate culturally affirming environments that support belonging and success.
  • They often do so with smaller endowments and less per-student funding than their outcomes warrant.

Campus climate

The attitudes, behaviors, and structures that shape whether students feel welcome and included. Climate directly affects retention and learning.

Student services & support

Advising, tutoring, counseling, cultural centers, disability services, and basic-needs support (food, housing) that remove barriers to success.

Validation & belonging

Rendón's validation theory and belonging research show that affirming interactions with faculty and staff are decisive for underserved students.

Leading change

Change & crisis management on campus

Universities are famously hard to change — loosely coupled, tradition-bound, and governed by consensus. John Kotter's eight-step model is a widely used lens for leading planned change; here it is applied to launching a campus-wide student-success initiative to raise retention.

Create a sense of urgency

Share the retention and equity-gap data widely so the community feels the problem is real and pressing.

Build a guiding coalition

Assemble a credible cross-unit team — faculty, advising, institutional research, student affairs — empowered to lead.

Form a strategic vision

Articulate a clear picture of the redesigned student experience and the outcomes it will produce.

Enlist a volunteer army

Communicate the vision relentlessly and recruit broad buy-in well beyond the coalition.

Enable action by removing barriers

Fix misaligned policies, data silos, and incentives that block advisors and faculty from acting.

Generate short-term wins

Pilot in one college, show an early persistence gain, and publicize it to sustain momentum.

Sustain acceleration

Scale the pilot, resist declaring victory too soon, and keep pressing on the harder cases.

Institute the change

Embed the practices in budget, job descriptions, and governance so they outlast the champions.

Change is not crisis

  • Planned change is deliberate and paced (Kotter; Kezar's models of change in higher education).
  • Crisis management is reactive — a campus emergency, scandal, or sudden budget shock.
  • Kezar stresses that change in higher ed is often political and cultural, not merely a linear sequence of steps.

Crisis leadership essentials

  • Prepare: a crisis plan, response team, and communication tree before you need them.
  • Communicate: fast, honest, single-source-of-truth messaging to every stakeholder.
  • Coordinate: an incident-command structure with clear decision authority.
  • Recover & learn: an after-action review that feeds back into policy.
Legal aspects of higher education

The law that governs the campus

Colleges operate inside a dense legal framework. These are the domains every administrator must recognize — the areas where a decision can create real legal exposure.

Assessment & academic programs

Proving and improving quality

Institutions demonstrate quality through a cycle of assessment, program review, and accreditation — internal improvement paired with external accountability.

Outcomes assessment

Are students learning?

  • Define student learning outcomes
  • Gather direct & indirect evidence
  • Interpret results
  • "Close the loop": use findings to improve
Program review

Is the program healthy?

  • Periodic self-study of a program
  • Curriculum, faculty, demand, outcomes, cost
  • External reviewers & site visit
  • Action plan for improvement or sunset
Accreditation

Does it meet standards?

  • Institutional (regional) & specialized/programmatic
  • Peer review against published standards
  • Gateway to federal financial aid
  • Self-study + external evaluation cycle
Accreditation is the gatekeeper. Recognized by the U.S. Department of Education and CHEA, institutional accreditation is what makes students eligible for Title IV federal aid — which is why it carries such weight in governance and quality assurance.
Accreditation · in depth

How quality is externally validated

Accreditation is voluntary peer review against published standards — but functionally mandatory, because Title IV federal aid flows only to accredited institutions. Three types operate at different levels, and each runs on a multi-year cycle.

Institutional (regional)

The whole institution

Historically organized by geographic region (e.g., SACSCOC, HLC, MSCHE, NECHE, WSCUC, NWCCU), these accreditors evaluate the entire institution and are the gateway to federal aid. Since 2020 the "regional" label is formally gone — institutions may seek any recognized institutional accreditor — but the legacy agencies still dominate.

National

By type, not place

National accreditors historically served faith-based, career, and for-profit institutions across the country. Credits from nationally accredited schools have often not transferred to institutionally/regionally accredited ones — a persistent equity and mobility concern.

Programmatic / specialized

A single program

Discipline-specific accreditors validate individual programs — ABET (engineering), AACSB (business), CCNE/ACEN (nursing), CAEP (education), LCME (medicine), ABA (law). Often required for professional licensure and employer recognition.

The accreditation cycle

Self-study

The institution documents how it meets each standard, gathering evidence over one to two years.

Peer review & site visit

A team of peer evaluators reviews the self-study and visits campus to verify and probe.

Commission action

The accreditor's commission grants, reaffirms, or conditions status (warning, probation, show-cause).

Ongoing monitoring

Interim reports, substantive-change reviews, and a reaffirmation cycle (commonly 7–10 years).

Assessment for accreditation

  • Accreditors require evidence of student learning outcomes assessment — not just inputs and intentions.
  • Institutions must show a closed loop: outcomes defined, measured, interpreted, and used to improve.
  • A culture of assessment — sustained, faculty-owned, and mission-aligned — is what reviewers look for, versus a scramble the year before a visit.
  • Regional/institutional accreditors increasingly emphasize student achievement: completion, retention, and post-graduation outcomes.
Assessment and accreditation are the same loop at two scales. Course- and program-level outcomes assessment rolls up into the institutional evidence an accreditor demands — which is why "close the loop" is the through-line from a single syllabus to a reaffirmation of accreditation.
Reference

Core vocabulary

  • Shared governance — the division of authority among board, administration, and faculty.
  • Provost — the chief academic officer; second-ranking executive after the president.
  • Tenure — an indefinite appointment protecting academic freedom, granted after peer review.
  • Tuition discounting — institutional aid that lowers sticker price to net price.
  • Land-grant — institutions funded under the Morrill Acts to broaden practical higher education.
  • Carnegie Classification — the standard framework for grouping U.S. institutions by mission.
  • MSI — Minority-Serving Institution (HBCU, HSI, TCU, AANAPISI, PBI).
  • Self-authorship — the internal capacity to define one's beliefs, identity, and relationships.
  • Title IV — the federal student-aid programs; eligibility depends on accreditation.
  • Loosely coupled system — Weick/Birnbaum's view of universities as weakly linked units.
